Novella Reader

A quieter reading experience designed to help people move from “I want to read” to a confident book choice—without making discovery feel like shopping.

01 / Overview

The opportunity

Discovery should feel like choosing a story—not clearing a storefront.

Reading platforms often ask one interface to do several jobs at once: sell books, surface reviews, manage a library, recommend titles, and host the reading experience. When every goal receives equal visual weight, readers must sort through competing carousels, ratings, promotions, and metadata before deciding what genuinely interests them.

Novella explores a more focused system. It uses mood and reading intent as an additional discovery layer, gives editorial content space to breathe, and reveals practical details when they become useful. The goal is not to remove information—it is to establish a clearer sequence for encountering it.

01

Discover by intent

Readers can begin with the kind of experience they want—comforting, tense, reflective, or unfamiliar—then refine by genre and format.

02

Evaluate without overload

Synopsis, tone, author context, and an optional excerpt arrive before deeper metadata and community signals.

03

Build a personal archive

Saved books, reading history, and collections are presented as a coherent library rather than another recommendation feed.

i

Project scope: Novella is a self-directed design concept, not a launched product. The comparative review and behavioral profiles informed design hypotheses; they are not substitutes for interviews or usability testing. The validation section outlines what I would test before committing to the product direction.

02 / Comparative review

Informal product audit

Three useful products reveal three different priorities.

I reviewed common flows in Kindle, Goodreads, and Libby to understand how each product handles discovery, decision-making, and library management. This was a directional interface review—not formal research or a universal assessment of the products.

Kindle

Commerce + reading
Useful strength A connected purchasing and reading ecosystem with familiar library access.
Design tension Shopping, subscriptions, recommendations, and the personal library compete for attention on the same surface.
Opportunity explored Separate focused discovery from the user’s owned and saved collection.

Goodreads

Catalog + community
Useful strength Extensive catalog information, reader reviews, shelves, and social proof.
Design tension Dense information can make it difficult to understand the book itself before encountering other people’s opinions.
Opportunity explored Introduce the work first, then let the reader choose when to view ratings and reviews.

Libby

Borrowing + reading
Useful strength A friendly borrowing experience with clear availability and library-card integration.
Design tension Availability and hold status can interrupt discovery after a reader has already formed interest.
Opportunity explored Keep availability visible while supporting wishlists, alternatives, and continued exploration.
Hypothesis 01

A smaller, more intentional recommendation set may help readers compare rather than continuously browse.

Hypothesis 02

Mood can complement genre when its language is concrete, understandable, and easy to override.

Hypothesis 03

Progressively revealing reviews and detailed metadata may protect exploration without withholding useful information.

03 / Audience

Behavior before personality labels

Two reading behaviors shaped the experience.

Instead of assigning MBTI or Enneagram types, I framed the audience around observable goals, habits, and friction. One person may move between both behaviors depending on the moment.

Atmospheric browser

“I know how I want a book to feel.”

This reader is open to discovery but does not want an endless list. They need enough context to imagine the experience before committing.

  • Starts with mood, setting, pacing, or emotional intensity.
  • Wants unfamiliar titles—not only repeated bestsellers.
  • Uses synopsis, samples, and author context to build confidence.
  • May abandon a search when recommendations feel repetitive or too broad.
Deliberate curator

“I want my library to remain useful.”

This reader values control, organization, and reliable reading settings. Discovery matters, but so does returning to previously saved work.

  • Organizes books by status, theme, project, or custom collection.
  • Needs predictable search, filters, and metadata.
  • Values typography controls and a focused reading environment.
  • Wants highlights and saved titles to remain portable and easy to find.
04 / Journey

Experience structure

From vague intention to an informed choice.

The proposed journey treats discovery as a sequence. Each stage answers a different question instead of placing the entire catalog, community, and purchase decision on one screen.

01

Set an intention

The reader selects a mood, pacing preference, genre, or format—or skips personalization entirely.

02

Browse a shortlist

A focused set of recommendations provides meaningful contrast rather than an endless feed.

03

Evaluate the work

Synopsis, tone, author context, details, and an optional blind excerpt support the decision.

04

Save or begin

The chosen book moves into the personal archive, where reading and organization become the priority.

05 / Decisions

Product and interaction design

Design choices tied to specific tasks.

Novella’s visual atmosphere supports the concept, but the structure is doing the heavier work. These decisions are meant to make the experience more understandable—not merely more decorative.

Discovery model

Mood supplements genre; it does not replace it.

Labels such as “reflective,” “fast-paced,” or “uneasy” offer another entry point while conventional filters remain visible and editable.

Reason

Emotional language can be useful but subjective. Pairing it with familiar metadata gives readers control.

Information hierarchy

Reveal detail when it becomes relevant.

The first view emphasizes the book’s identity, premise, and tone. Reviews, publication data, and deeper metadata remain one step away.

Reason

This protects the initial encounter without hiding information needed for a considered decision.

Optional experiment

Let prose speak before the cover does.

The “blind excerpt” concept lets a reader sample a short passage before seeing the title, cover, ratings, or author identity.

Reason

It could surface unexpected matches—but should remain optional and be tested for confusion, trust, and accessibility.

Personal archive

Separate ownership from recommendation.

The library prioritizes saved, active, and completed books rather than using the user’s collection as another promotional feed.

Reason

Returning users need reliable access to their own content more than another round of discovery.

07 / Desktop direction

Exploring density

Two directions tested the balance between browsing and focus.

I explored two responsive-homepage directions. The comparison helped clarify the product’s visual priority, but it does not prove which design would perform better without usability testing.

Direction A

Library grid

Supports high-volume scanning and quick access to many titles, but risks giving every item equal emphasis.

Direction B · Selected hypothesis

Editorial spotlight

Creates a clearer entry point and stronger visual identity, while requiring deliberate access to the broader catalog.

Why I carried Direction B forward

It more directly supports Novella’s differentiation: a focused introduction to a small number of books. In a real product process, I would test both directions on discovery speed, comprehension, perceived control, and the ability to reach the full library—not select the editorial version on aesthetics alone.

08 / Visual system

Editorial but functional

A literary identity that preserves interface clarity.

Warm neutrals, burgundy accents, generous spacing, and an editorial serif give the product a distinct voice. A neutral sans-serif carries controls and metadata so the atmosphere does not compromise usability.

Core palette

Warm surfaces provide continuity while burgundy is reserved for emphasis and action.

09 / Validation plan

What I would test next

Turn the visual concept into an evidence-based product direction.

The largest open question is whether the proposed atmosphere improves decision-making or simply looks appealing. I would test the core assumptions before expanding the feature set.

1

Mood-language comprehension

Ask readers to interpret and use mood labels, then identify ambiguous terms and where genre or content filters remain necessary.

2

Discovery task success

Measure whether participants can find a plausible next book, explain why it fits, and recover when the first recommendation misses.

3

Desktop direction comparison

Compare the grid and spotlight designs on discoverability, perceived choice, time to useful information, and access to the full catalog.

4

Reading and accessibility checks

Test contrast, dynamic type, keyboard and screen-reader use, reduced motion, reading controls, and long-form comfort on real devices.

10 / Reflection

What the project demonstrates

Atmosphere is strongest when the structure earns it.

Novella began as a visual exploration, but the more useful design problem became sequencing: what should a reader encounter first, what can wait, and how should discovery differ from managing an owned library? The project strengthened my ability to connect art direction with information architecture while remaining honest about what still needs validation.

01

Strongest contribution

A cohesive visual and interaction direction that connects discovery, evaluation, author context, and collection management.

02

Largest product risk

Mood-based personalization can become vague, reductive, or repetitive unless readers can understand, edit, and escape it.

03

Next design step

Prototype the core discovery flow, test it with readers, and revise the labels and hierarchy before adding social or recommendation complexity.
