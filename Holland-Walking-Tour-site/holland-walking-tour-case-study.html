01 · Executive brief

The strategic problem, product direction, ownership, and evidence boundary.

The real problem was not digitizing a map. It was creating a service people could trust.

Holland’s walking-tour experience relied on static materials that were difficult to read outdoors, update consistently, and follow while moving. The opportunity connected two forms of trust: visitor confidence in the route and organizational confidence in the historical content behind it.

Strategic problem

Independent exploration breaks down when visitors cannot judge a route’s demands, recover from change, or trust that a stop’s information is current.

Product direction

Connect route discovery and in-motion guidance with a reusable publishing system instead of treating the visitor app as an isolated set of screens.

My scope

Research synthesis, information architecture, end-to-end flows, high-fidelity mobile UI, accessibility requirements, and an administrative-system concept.

Evidence boundary

The work is a researched product direction and high-fidelity concept—not a production launch. Adoption, revenue, and city-foot-traffic outcomes were not measured.

Three principles aligned the work

They became shared criteria for prioritizing the experience—not slogans added after the interface was complete.

01 · Before movement

Make commitment legible.

Expose time, distance, difficulty, accessibility, stops, and offline readiness before asking someone to begin.

02 · During movement

Minimize demanded attention.

Prioritize one next action, visible progress, and recovery over dense maps or prolonged screen engagement.

03 · Behind the interface

Treat content integrity as UX.

Design the records, roles, and publishing states required to keep public history accurate and reusable.

02 · Research

Finding the recurring barriers behind outdoor navigation and historical discovery.

Research translated into product requirements and risk priorities.

Feedback from visitors, older adults, and project collaborators pointed to three recurring needs: outdoor legibility, confidence about the commitment ahead, and freedom to explore without feeling trapped in a rigid itinerary.

28 Participants and collaborators represented in the research synthesis, including older local residents, younger visitors, and city stakeholders.

Traceability from signal to decision

I organized recurring comments and frustrations into an affinity map, identified the experience risk behind each pattern, and translated it into a requirement the prototype could make visible and testable.

How evidence moved into the product
Research signal Experience risk Product requirement Interface response
“Paper maps are difficult to read in bright sunlight.” Visitors lose orientation or stop engaging when information cannot be scanned outdoors. Preserve legibility under glare, motion, and one-handed use. High contrast, 16px minimum body type, short content layers, and generous targets.
“I want to know how long the tour takes and where I’m going.” An unclear commitment creates anxiety before the tour and uncertainty during it. Expose route demands before start and maintain progress visibility throughout. Duration, distance, difficulty, ordered stops, route preview, and next-stop status.
“I want to explore—not feel like I’m following instructions.” A rigid itinerary reduces autonomy and makes the experience feel procedural. Support both trusted curation and visitor-controlled discovery. Curated routes plus a custom builder with explicit selection and a live estimate.

Evidence note: the synthesis is directional. It established prototype priorities; it does not establish population-level preference or post-launch impact.

03 · Experience structure

A straightforward route from choosing a tour to understanding each stop.

Reducing decisions while users are already in motion.

The primary flow keeps the most important choices early, before a visitor begins walking. Once the tour starts, each screen focuses on the next action: follow the route, recognize the stop, and view its story.

  1. Step 01

    Open the app

    Orient the visitor and introduce available tour options.

  2. Step 02

    Choose a route

    Select a curated tour or begin building a custom one.

  3. Step 03

    Review details

    See stops, duration, distance, and expected difficulty.

  4. Step 04

    Begin walking

    Follow clear directions and visible route progress.

  5. Step 05

    Discover a stop

    Open concise historical context, imagery, and details.

  6. Step 06

    Continue or finish

    Move to the next stop or complete the tour confidently.

Custom route branch: visitors can browse available landmarks, select preferred stops, and review an updated route estimate before beginning.

The trust test is what happens off the happy path

These resilience states would need product, content, and engineering agreement before implementation—and field testing before release.

Location unavailable

Keep orientation possible.

Preserve the itinerary and landmark cues, explain the limitation, and offer manual stop progression instead of presenting a dead map.

Network interrupted

Make offline truth explicit.

Distinguish downloaded route content from unavailable media and keep the visitor’s progress intact.

Route deviation

Recover without blame.

Explain the new path, show its effect on time and distance, and allow return, reroute, or skip without losing context.

Needs change mid-tour

Adapt the commitment.

Support pausing, step-free alternatives, rest amenities, a shorter route, or a deliberate end state.

04 · Design decisions

Tradeoffs, visual-system logic, and the quality criteria required for delivery.

The visual system was treated as a quality contract—not surface styling.

The visual language references Holland’s waterfront identity while prioritizing outdoor use. Each decision balances product character with legibility, attention, accessibility, and the implementation rules needed to keep the experience consistent.

Information hierarchy

Commitment before immersion

Tradeoff: more pre-tour metadata adds density, but structured scanning prevents surprise around time, distance, difficulty, and access.

In-motion interaction

Glanceability over map richness

Tradeoff: a restrained map shows less at once, but one dominant instruction, progress, and landmark context reduce attention demanded by the phone.

Historical content

Layer depth without hiding access

Tradeoff: concise stories omit detail, so summaries are paired with imagery, audio, transcripts, and optional depth instead of one long reading path.

Proposed delivery criteria

Design quality must be testable by the team building and maintaining it.

Accessibility baseline

Readable type, 44px targets, visible focus, sufficient contrast, transcripts, and reduced-motion support.

Responsive behavior

Core tasks remain usable across narrow mobile and wide administrative layouts; no essential action depends on hover.

Content standards

Plain-language summaries, source metadata, descriptive alternatives, and consistent fields for every stop.

State coverage

Loading, empty, offline, permission, error, and recovery behavior are reviewed alongside the ideal journey.

05 · Mobile solution

Key screens from browsing and customizing a tour through navigating its stops.

The interface keeps the city—not the app—at the center.

The redesigned mobile journey moves from discovery to reflection without asking visitors to make abstract choices too early. Route commitment, accessibility, offline support, and next actions stay visible when they matter, while the interface remains quiet enough to keep attention on the city.

One journey, not a collection of screens. The system carries the same route data, accessibility language, visual hierarchy, and sense of place from planning through completion. Select any image to view it larger.

Plan with confidence

Visitors can understand the experience, compare its demands, personalize the route, and confirm the plan before location-based guidance begins.

01 · Discover

Show value before asking for access.

Tour options, useful metadata, accessibility filters, and nearby experiences replace the blocking introductory carousel.

02 · Assess

Make the commitment visible.

Time, distance, difficulty, stop count, step-free access, audio availability, and offline support appear before Start tour.

03 · Personalize

Turn selection into informed choice.

Checkmarks and labels clarify selection, while the running time and distance update before the route is created.

04 · Confirm

Offer one last spatial check.

The numbered itinerary matches the route map and exposes restrooms, benches, walk times, and offline readiness.

Walk with support

During the tour, the interface becomes more glanceable and resilient—supporting orientation, audio learning, fatigue, weather, and route changes.

05 · Navigate

Design for a glance, not a stare.

One large instruction, a restrained map, progress, offline status, and the next landmark reduce attention demanded by the phone.

06 · Listen

Layer the story instead of presenting a wall of text.

Photography, audio, transcript access, short-form history, and a visual observation prompt support different learning preferences.

07 · Adapt

Let the route respond to real life.

Visitors can pause, reroute, find rest amenities, prepare for weather, skip a stop, or end deliberately without losing context.

08 · Reflect

Celebrate meaningfully, without gamifying history.

The recap records what was experienced and offers optional saving, sharing, reflection, and continued exploration.

06 · Content system

Connecting responsive web UI, content governance, and the people responsible for public history.

The administrative system makes content maintenance part of product quality.

I explored a web-based administrative model for creating tours, managing historical stops, and preserving media. This shifted the work from a mobile interface toward a broader service with connected visitor, editorial, archival, and publishing needs.

Proposed service model

One shared library, many possible tours.

Rather than duplicating a landmark every time it appears in a route, the proposed structure treats each stop as a reusable record. Tour managers can then sequence those records into different seasonal, historical, or thematic experiences.

Content integrity

Reusable stop records

Maintain one source for a landmark’s text, media, location, and archival information.

Publishing safety

Draft before live

Give archivists a clear review step before edited content becomes visible to visitors.

Scalability

Flexible tour assembly

Support future tours by reusing and resequencing existing stops rather than rebuilding content.

A governance loop—not only a dashboard

This operating model is a design hypothesis for stakeholder validation. It makes ownership and publishing safety explicit before implementation.

  1. 01 · Compose

    Assemble a route

    A tour manager creates a draft by selecting and sequencing reusable stop records.

  2. 02 · Review

    Verify the source

    An archivist reviews copy, media, attribution, location, and accessibility information.

  3. 03 · Publish

    Approve a version

    An authorized owner confirms readiness and controls when the route becomes public.

  4. 04 · Learn

    Improve the source

    The team reviews support and engagement signals, then updates the shared record—not duplicate copies.

01 · Tour management

Plan and publish routes

Review active tours, create drafts, organize stops, and control when a route is visible.

02 · Stop library

Maintain reusable landmarks

Search and organize the central set of landmarks that can be added to more than one tour.

03 · Media library

Preserve visual context

Store historical images and supporting metadata so the public experience remains traceable and accurate.

04 · Engagement view

Identify experience issues

07 · Measurement & validation

Defining the evidence required to move from a strong concept toward a responsible release.

Define success before claiming impact.

A polished prototype is not proof that the service works. I would evaluate visitor confidence, in-motion resilience, content integrity, and operational sustainability with measures tied to the riskiest assumptions in the model.

Visitor decision quality

Can people choose an appropriate route?

Test whether route information supports an informed commitment before movement begins.

  • Route-selection task completion without assistance
  • Confidence rating before starting the tour
  • Where and why visitors abandon before start
In-motion resilience

Can people recover when conditions change?

Evaluate the moments most likely to break trust outdoors rather than testing only the ideal route.

  • Time and success rate for route recovery
  • Comprehension during GPS or network interruption
  • Successful pause, skip, shorten, and end actions
Content integrity

Can the public information stay trustworthy?

Measure the quality and safety of the publishing workflow behind the visitor experience.

  • Time from requested correction to approved update
  • Duplicate or stale stop records identified
  • Issues caught during review before publication
Operational sustainability

Can the service grow without multiplying work?

Assess whether the reusable content model reduces effort while preserving accountability.

  • Time to assemble a route from existing stops
  • Stop reuse across seasonal or thematic tours
  • Maintainer confidence and support burden
Phase 01 · Risk-first field test

Walk the route

Moderated sessions in the real environment would examine glare, landmark recognition, one-handed use, route deviation, fatigue, and comprehension.

Phase 02 · Quality review

Stress the interface

Review screen-reader and keyboard access, contrast, reduced motion, permissions, low connectivity, content variants, and recovery states.

Phase 03 · Limited pilot

Learn before scaling

Pilot one route, instrument a privacy-conscious funnel, collect maintainer feedback, and revise the service model before expanding.

These measures are a validation plan, not reported results. No adoption, completion-rate, accessibility-compliance, or operational-efficiency claim is made in this case study.
08 · Reflection

The strategic shift, decision discipline, and delivery thinking demonstrated by the work.

The product became stronger when I designed the operating model—not only the screens.

The most important shift was treating the walking tour as a connected service. Visitor confidence, accessible interaction, trustworthy content, publishing responsibility, and implementation constraints all shape the same experience.

Product framing

One service, two connected experiences

The visitor journey and the publishing workflow share content, quality standards, and responsibility instead of operating as separate products.

Decision quality

Evidence, risk, requirement, response

The reasoning chain makes design decisions reviewable and gives future testing a specific hypothesis to challenge.

Delivery readiness

States, entities, governance, and QA criteria

The content model, resilience states, publishing loop, and quality contract create a stronger bridge from concept to implementation.
