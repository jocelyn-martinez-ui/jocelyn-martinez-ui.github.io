Holland Walking Tour

I helped shape an accessible mobile walking-tour experience for Holland, Michigan—and explored the content system local teams would need to keep its historical stories accurate, flexible, and easy to maintain.

01 · Overview

The product challenge, my contribution, and the project’s practical scope.

Turning a paper tour into a more flexible city experience.

Holland’s walking-tour experience relied on static materials that could be difficult to read, update, and follow while moving through the city. The design opportunity was to make local history easier to discover without losing the character and context that made the tour meaningful.

Challenge

Create a tour experience that supports independent exploration while remaining clear for visitors who may be unfamiliar with the city or less comfortable with mobile technology.

My contribution

I contributed to research synthesis, user flows, interface design, accessibility decisions, and the technical thinking behind a maintainable content structure.

Primary users

Visitors taking a self-guided tour, including older adults; local archivists and tourism stakeholders responsible for maintaining accurate information.

Deliverables

Mobile user flows, high-fidelity tour screens, an administrative interface concept, and a scalable content model for stops that can appear across multiple tours.

02 · Research

Finding the recurring barriers behind outdoor navigation and historical discovery.

Research showed that clarity mattered as much as content.

Feedback from visitors, older adults, and project collaborators pointed to three recurring needs: readable information in outdoor conditions, confidence about where a route would lead, and freedom to discover the city without feeling locked into a rigid itinerary.

28 Participants and collaborators represented in the research synthesis, including older local residents, younger visitors, and city stakeholders.

From observations to design priorities

I organized recurring comments and frustrations into an affinity map, then translated those patterns into interface requirements: larger readable type, visible route details, clear progress, simple tour choices, and flexible stop selection.

Outdoor legibility

“Paper maps are difficult to read in bright sunlight.”

Design response: strong contrast, a minimum 16px body size, concise copy, and large interaction targets.

Route confidence

“I want to know how long the tour takes and where I’m going.”

Design response: surface duration, distance, route difficulty, progress, and upcoming stops before the user begins.

Independent discovery

“I want to explore—not feel like I’m following instructions.”

Design response: support both curated routes and a custom route built from stops the visitor chooses.

03 · Experience structure

A straightforward route from choosing a tour to understanding each stop.

Reducing decisions while users are already in motion.

The primary flow keeps the most important choices early, before a visitor begins walking. Once the tour starts, each screen focuses on the next action: follow the route, recognize the stop, and view its story.

Step 01

Open the app

Orient the visitor and introduce available tour options.

Step 02

Choose a route

Select a curated tour or begin building a custom one.

Step 03

Review details

See stops, duration, distance, and expected difficulty.

Step 04

Begin walking

Follow clear directions and visible route progress.

Step 05

Discover a stop

Open concise historical context, imagery, and details.

Step 06

Continue or finish

Move to the next stop or complete the tour confidently.

Custom route branch: visitors can browse available landmarks, select preferred stops, and review an updated route estimate before beginning.
04 · Design decisions

Visual choices tied directly to outdoor use, accessibility, and local identity.

A calm system designed to stay readable outdoors.

The visual language references Holland’s waterfront identity without relying on decorative themes. Color, typography, spacing, and interaction size were selected to support quick scanning and comfortable use while walking.

Typography

Readable before decorative

Body copy begins at 16px, headings are clearly differentiated, and content is broken into short sections so users can scan without stopping for long.

Interaction

Forgiving touch targets

Primary actions use generous spacing and targets of approximately 44 × 44px or larger to reduce errors while users are holding and moving with a phone.

Navigation

Confidence at every step

Route duration, distance, progress, and next-stop information remain visible where they can reduce uncertainty and prevent accidental exits.

05 · Mobile solution

Key screens from browsing and customizing a tour through navigating its stops.

The interface keeps the city—not the app—at the center.

The redesigned mobile journey moves from discovery to reflection without asking visitors to make abstract choices too early. Route commitment, accessibility, offline support, and next actions stay visible when they matter, while the interface remains quiet enough to keep attention on the city.

One journey, not a collection of screens. The system carries the same route data, accessibility language, visual hierarchy, and sense of place from planning through completion. Select any image to view it larger.

Plan with confidence

Visitors can understand the experience, compare its demands, personalize the route, and confirm the plan before location-based guidance begins.

01 · Discover

Show value before asking for access.

Tour options, useful metadata, accessibility filters, and nearby experiences replace the blocking introductory carousel.

02 · Assess

Make the commitment visible.

Time, distance, difficulty, stop count, step-free access, audio availability, and offline support appear before Start tour.

03 · Personalize

Turn selection into informed choice.

Checkmarks and labels clarify selection, while the running time and distance update before the route is created.

04 · Confirm

Offer one last spatial check.

The numbered itinerary matches the route map and exposes restrooms, benches, walk times, and offline readiness.

Walk with support

During the tour, the interface becomes more glanceable and resilient—supporting orientation, audio learning, fatigue, weather, and route changes.

05 · Navigate

Design for a glance, not a stare.

One large instruction, a restrained map, progress, offline status, and the next landmark reduce attention demanded by the phone.

06 · Listen

Layer the story instead of presenting a wall of text.

Photography, audio, transcript access, short-form history, and a visual observation prompt support different learning preferences.

07 · Adapt

Let the route respond to real life.

Visitors can pause, reroute, find rest amenities, prepare for weather, skip a stop, or end deliberately without losing context.

08 · Reflect

Celebrate meaningfully, without gamifying history.

The recap records what was experienced and offers optional saving, sharing, reflection, and continued exploration.

06 · Content system

Designing beyond the visitor-facing app to support the people responsible for its content.

A tour is only useful if its stories can stay accurate.

I also explored an administrative system for creating tours, managing historical stops, and preserving media. This shifted the project from a set of screens toward a broader service: one experience for visitors and another for the people maintaining it.

Administrative concept

One shared library, many possible tours.

Rather than duplicating a landmark every time it appears in a route, the proposed structure treats each stop as a reusable record. Tour managers can then sequence those records into different seasonal, historical, or thematic experiences.

Content integrity

Reusable stop records

Maintain one source for a landmark’s text, media, location, and archival information.

Publishing safety

Draft before live

Give archivists a clear review step before edited content becomes visible to visitors.

Scalability

Flexible tour assembly

Support future tours by reusing and resequencing existing stops rather than rebuilding content.

01 · Tour management

Plan and publish routes

Review active tours, create drafts, organize stops, and control when a route is visible.

02 · Stop library

Maintain reusable landmarks

Search and organize the central set of landmarks that can be added to more than one tour.

03 · Media library

Preserve visual context

Store historical images and supporting metadata so the public experience remains traceable and accurate.

04 · Engagement view

Identify experience issues

A proposed dashboard for spotting where visitors may abandon a tour or encounter confusing stops.

07 · Reflection

What the project demonstrated and what I would validate next.

Designing the visible interface and the system behind it.

This project helped me connect my computer science background with UX practice. I learned to treat accessibility, content structure, and implementation constraints as part of the design problem—not details to resolve after the interface was complete.

What I demonstrated

Research translated into interface decisions

Participant concerns became concrete requirements for type size, route visibility, touch-target sizing, and flexible tour choices.

What I learned

Front-stage and back-stage UX are connected

A polished visitor experience depends on whether local teams can maintain trustworthy content without unnecessary duplication or risk.

What I would test next

Outdoor usability in the real environment

I would run a moderated walking test to evaluate screen glare, GPS uncertainty, route recovery, stop recognition, and one-handed use.

Project scope: This case study presents the researched product direction, high-fidelity mobile interfaces, and an administrative-system concept. Future validation would be needed before claiming adoption, commercial impact, or improvements to city foot traffic.
